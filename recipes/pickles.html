Instructions

  1. Wash and chill the cucumbers 2 h

    Wash the cucumbers. If they are not freshly picked, soak them in very icy water for at least 2 hours, then drain and pat dry.

    Small, firm pickling cucumbers — Kirby or Persian — have denser flesh and thinner skins, so they stay crisp. Avoid waxed salad cucumbers.

  2. Make the brine 5 min

    Mix of filtered water with of unrefined sea salt, and stir until the salt is completely dissolved. Set aside.

  3. Cut the spears 10 min

    Cut off the stem end of each cucumber and discard it. Slice the cucumbers lengthwise into quarters. You need for this many jars.

    Also trim about 1.5 mm off the blossom end. Moulds and tissue-softening enzymes live in the blossom area, and this is the single cheapest way to keep spears snappy.

  4. Layer the jar 10 min

    Put the spices and garlic in the bottom of each clean 1 L wide-mouth mason jar — , , of pickling spice, and of dill seeds. Pack the spears in tightly, stem-end down. Break each bay leaf in two and tuck the halves between the spears.

    Packing tightly matters: it stops the spears floating once the brine goes in.

  5. Add the brine 2.5 cm headspace

    Pour the brine over the cucumbers until they are completely covered, leaving 2.5 cm of headspace at the top for expansion. If you need more liquid, mix another batch of brine at exactly the same ratio.

  6. Seal the jar 2 min

    Screw the lid on tightly. If you are using an airlock lid, fill it with water according to its instructions.

  7. Ferment 2–3 days · 18–22 °C

    Leave at room temperature (18–22 °C) for . Start tasting after day 2 — the pickles are done when they taste good to you.

  8. Store fridge

    Remove the airlock lid if you used one and replace it with a storage lid. Move the jars to cold storage — the cold stops fermentation and preserves the pickles. Expect them to keep for months in the fridge.

Tips for perfectly crisp spears

  • Trim the blossom end. About 1.5 mm off each cucumber — that is where the softening enzymes sit.
  • Use tannin-rich leaves. A grape, horseradish or oak leaf, or a bay leaf, binds with the cucumber's pectin and keeps the spears snappy.
  • Choose the right cucumbers. Small, firm Kirby or Persian cucumbers hold up best.
  • Use the right salt. Pickling salt, sea salt or kosher salt — never iodised table salt, which clouds the brine and interferes with fermentation. Do not use salt with anti-caking agents.
  • Keep them submerged. A fermentation weight, or a small zip-top bag filled with brine, holds every spear below the brine line.

Troubleshooting

  • Cloudy brine and bubbles: normal signs of healthy fermentation. Carry on.
  • White film (kahm yeast): harmless. Skim it off and make sure the cucumbers stay submerged.
  • Fuzzy mould — green, black or pink: discard the entire batch. It is not safe to eat.
  • Soft or slimy pickles: spoilage. Discard the batch.

When in doubt, throw it out. Fizzy and sour is good; fuzzy, slimy or rotten-smelling is not.

Brine strength, and why it is fixed

  • Salt is the safety-critical number, so it is never scaled below the ratio you selected. More jars means more brine, not weaker brine.
  • Percentages here are salt ÷ (salt + water) by weight. Some recipes quote salt ÷ water alone, which reads higher — that is why 18 g / 532 mL is called a 3.5% brine by its source and reads ≈ 3.3% here.
  • Always mix extra brine at the same ratio rather than topping up with plain water.
  • Larger jars, warmer rooms and softer cucumbers all ferment faster. Cold and salt are the two brakes.
